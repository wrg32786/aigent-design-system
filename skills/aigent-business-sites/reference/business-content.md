# Business content

Use when the page has to sell, convince, book, or sign someone up. Adapted from Ultra Web Design, see `../NOTICE.md`.

## Pick one business job per surface

generate qualified leads, start a trial or account, complete a purchase or booking, request a demo or consultation, explain a complex product, establish trust, grow an audience or subscription, recruit, support existing customers, or send people to a place or event.

Secondary jobs can exist. They do not get to compete visually with the primary action.

## Visitor questions

Organize content around what the visitor needs to know, in whatever order fits their awareness:

1. **Orientation** what is this and who is it for?
2. **Relevance** does it fit my situation?
3. **Mechanism** how does it actually work?
4. **Evidence** why should I believe it?
5. **Experience** what is using, buying, or working with it like?
6. **Difference** why this instead of an alternative or doing nothing?
7. **Risk** limits, costs, obligations, protections?
8. **Action** what happens next?

Do not force every site into Hero, Features, Pricing, FAQ, CTA.

## Claims

Every important claim goes **claim, mechanism, proof, implication**. A claim with no mechanism reads inflated. A mechanism with no implication reads technical. Proof with no context can mislead.

## Audience by decisions, not demographics

Write down what triggers the visit, what they already know, the words they use, who approves the purchase, the risk they fear, the alternative they compare against, the evidence they trust, and their device, language, and connectivity constraints.

## Awareness state

| State | Need | Page behavior |
| --- | --- | --- |
| problem unaware | recognition | show the situation without overselling |
| problem aware | framing | why current workarounds fail |
| solution aware | mechanism and difference | show how this approach works |
| product aware | proof, risk, fit | compare, answer objections, show real usage |
| most aware | transaction clarity | price, availability, action, next step |

Do not open a pricing visitor with a manifesto. Do not ask a cold visitor to "Start now" before they understand the category.

## CTA rules

- One primary CTA per surface. A secondary is fine for a different readiness level, like "View demo" next to "Start trial".
- Labels name the next step: `Book a 20-minute review`, `Get the estimate`.
- Say what happens after high-friction actions.
- Repeat the CTA after major proof or an answered objection, not mechanically every section.
- One intent, one label. Do not express the same action three different ways.

## Proof ladder

Strongest to weakest:

1. direct product behavior or live demo
2. verifiable outcome with context and method
3. detailed case study
4. attributed customer quote
5. recognizable customer or partner, with permission
6. process transparency, credential, or third-party review
7. founder assertion

When logos, quotes, and metrics do not exist yet, use a product walkthrough, process transparency, clearly labeled sample output, team credentials, method and limits, public docs or source, transparent pricing, or a low-risk trial. Never fill a design hole with invented trust.

## Objections

For each main audience, record objection, type (fit, value, risk, effort, risk reversal), the answer or evidence, and which surface carries it.

## Success metric

One primary metric plus a few diagnostics: qualified lead rate (not just form starts), CTA click-through, step abandonment, performance and accessibility regressions, search impressions when SEO matters. Do not optimize a proxy that hurts qualified outcomes.

## Page grammars

Pick one dominant grammar and one supporting. They are relationships, not section orders.

| Grammar | Good for | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| editorial spine | services, thought leadership, complex products | style with no product proof |
| product theater | SaaS, hardware, tools | fake UI, too much animation |
| field guide / manual | technical, operational, educational | everything turns into docs |
| catalog / collection | ecommerce, portfolios, media | interchangeable card grid |
| manifesto / poster | launches, cultural brands, events | vague, inaccessible type |
| dossier / case file | B2B, security, research | density with no hierarchy |
| spatial journey | hospitality, real estate, experience brands | heavy media, lost orientation |
| product lab | technical or scientific products | decorative pseudo-science |
| command console | operational products | dashboard cosplay on a marketing page |
| journal / story | founder brands, media, community | weak path to purchase |

Hero options: product-first demo, proof-first result, problem-first scene, mechanism diagram, editorial headline with evidence, split narrative, catalog opening with a featured item, service promise with concrete scope, or a cinematic opening for experience mode. Skip the centered headline, vague subhead, two pills, and floating dashboard unless the brief earns it.

## By business type

**SaaS and software.** Real product behavior, role and use-case specifics, integrations and limits, time to value, security and data handling, clear plan differences. Common rut: indigo gradient, glass panels, generic bento, invented 10x claims, identical icon cards.

**Ecommerce.** Consistent product imagery, price, availability, variants, delivery, returns, total cost, materials, dimensions, compatibility, honest reviews only, stock and delay states. Never hide purchase info behind decorative interaction. Structured data only when accurate and visible.

**Editorial, newsletter, community.** Reading measure and hierarchy, recency, authorship, sourcing, taxonomy, archive and search, ad and sponsor distinction, transcripts. Do not interrupt reading with a signup before value shows up. Rut: same card and same weight for every story.

**Service and local business.** Exact scope, geography, availability, client fit, process and what the client has to do, named team, work samples, pricing model when possible, qualified inquiry path, phone, hours, and address. Rut: stock-photo hero, vague "solutions", icon grid, unqualified contact form.

**Portfolio and agency.** Work before self-description, role and contribution per project, outcome and constraints, contact availability, fast media. Rut: a long motion intro that delays the work.

**Course, event, membership.** Who it is for, curriculum or schedule, host credibility, format, dates, time commitment, prerequisites, price, refund and cancellation, honest outcomes.

**Nonprofit and public service.** Mission turned into a specific action, program evidence, financial transparency, donation use and receipts, plain language, low bandwidth, multilingual and assistive needs.

## Navigation and footer

- Nav labels use audience language, not the org chart. Keep the primary set scannable. Show current location.
- Mobile nav is not a squashed mega-menu. Do not hide the primary action inside it when it needs to stay visible.
- Breadcrumbs for deep hierarchies only. Search when content scale justifies it.
- The footer is a continuation surface, not a graveyard: key paths, contact, legal, privacy, accessibility, socials the business actually runs, and a newsletter only if it has distinct value.
