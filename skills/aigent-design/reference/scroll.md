# Scroll

Load when the page is a scroll story: pinned chapters, scrubbed video, layered heroes, lateral rails, or any surface where scroll position drives what the visitor sees. Ordinary reveal-on-entry pages only need `motion.md`.

Adapted from `nateherkai/scroll-craft` and `nutlope/hallmark` (both MIT). See `../NOTICE.md`.

## Plan the feeling before the sections

A scroll page is a sequence of states someone passes through with a hand on the wheel. Write these into the direction before choosing a single device:

- **Journey**: four to seven beats, each a shift in what the visitor knows (recognition, tension, turn, substance, range, commitment). A section that serves no beat is cut.
- **Feeling curve**: one line per section, the emotion first and the on-screen cause second. Two adjacent sections with the same feeling means one is filler.
- **One peak**: the moment a visitor would describe to a friend, written as that sentence. It gets the best asset, a quieter section before it, and the most scroll distance on the page. Three impressive sections flatten each other into none.
- **The tell-someone test**: complete "it's the site where ___" with something that happened to the visitor, not a device name. "It has a scrub video" fails.
- **A resolved ending**: the last screen holds content and stops. Fading into a footer overwrites the peak.

Silence is allowed only when it is authored. Note any intentional empty viewport in the direction so QA can tell it from dead scroll.

## Pick a structure on purpose

Variety of effect is not variety of page. Choose the structural grammar first; nav, hero, and close follow from it.

| Grammar | Fits | Refuses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Filmic one-shot | one linear argument, consumer product | visible sequence counters, chapter indexes |
| Chaptered editorial | method, manifesto, long-form substance | continuous colour drift; each chapter is a hard change |
| Live surface | software where the demo is the argument | marketing chrome, fake dashboards; the product UI is real |
| Continuous world | real geography or physical process | section boundaries; it is the expensive, fragile option |
| Typographic poster | a brand whose asset is a sentence | photographic grounds, scrims |
| Gallery / catalog | a range, a portfolio, a menu | a single hero claim, argument-shaped pinned type |
| Split stage | two sides: before/after, manual/automated | full-bleed media before the resolve, centred copy |
| Rhythmic cutlist | energy brands, events, sport | long pins, dwell, parallax; the peak is the one held frame |

Filmic one-shot is the habitual default. Choosing it should come with a reason the others lost.

## Device families

Assign a device per beat, never per page. A good scroll page uses at least four families and never the same family twice in a row.

| Family | What scroll does | Tuning that matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| scrub | video or frame sequence follows the wheel | 2.2 to 3 viewport-heights per hero; at most two scrub sections per page |
| pin | the frame holds while content states change | span at least about 1.2 viewports, or progress snaps; overlap copy states by about 15% |
| pan | vertical scroll drives lateral travel | about one viewport per item plus one; measure `scrollWidth - innerWidth`, aim for at least half a viewport of overflow |
| reveal | a clip-path wipe marks a change of state | use it big; an iris at most once per page |
| kinetic | a heading assembles by line or word | lines almost always; one kinetic head per section; re-split after the font stylesheet's load event and a non-empty `document.fonts.load()` for each face (see `type.md`), not on `document.fonts.ready` |
| parallax | layers move at different rates | adjacent planes differ by 10 to 30%; copy rides at 1x; never body text on a moving layer |
| count | a real number lands | 1.2 to 1.8s, hard ease-out, fires once at half visibility; no verified figure means no counter |
| flow | ordinary sections with reveal-once | 30 to 80ms stagger, roughly 14px rise; content never re-hides on scroll-up |
| pointer | tilt, magnet, light follows the cursor | gated to `(hover: hover) and (pointer: fine)`; magnet on the primary action only |
| drift | the page ground interpolates between sections | three to five small stops in one colour family; paint hard edges instead when several short sections overlap |

One thing is the reason each section exists. Everything else in it is support.

## Mechanics that break quietly

These failures look correct in any single screenshot.

- **Clip time vs. pin time.** A pinned stage is visible for a viewport before its pin starts and after it ends. Map clip time across the stage's whole visible life, not only the pinned travel, or the film freezes while a still frame slides past. On a phone, end a scrub where its object leaves the screen. One object went under the header a fifth of a screen into a scrub that ran for three quarters of one, so a quarter of its box showed at the scrub's midpoint on a 390 x 844 phone and none at its end. Shoot the phone's first screen and the object's whole scrub before review.
- **Lerp the playhead.** Scroll sets a target time; a requestAnimationFrame loop eases toward it (around 0.18 per frame), skips sub-frame seeks, and never queues a seek while one is resolving. Writing `currentTime` directly on every scroll event stutters. Clamp the target to the stage's range and stop the loop once it settles. Let resize, load, and pageshow wake the loop rather than snap the playhead to the target: phones fire resize as the address bar hides mid-scroll, and a slow network fires load while the visitor is already scrolling, so a snap on either puts the jump back. Snap only on a jump of more than about a tenth of the run, such as a link back to the start, which would otherwise play the story backwards in front of the visitor.
- **An eased layer snaps while it is off screen.** A drawn layer that eases toward scroll progress only while its section is visible goes stale after a jump past it. Nav links and buttons jump without smooth scrolling: one eased value stayed near a fifth of its range after any of them, and the parts gated on it stayed hidden until about thirty scroll events later, when they popped in whole. Set the value straight to its target whenever the section is off screen, where nobody sees the snap, and update it from the observer's callback as well, so the snap does not wait for a scroll. Test every in-page link from the top of the page and look at once.
- **Encode for seeking.** Scrubbed video needs a dense keyframe interval (a keyframe every 4 to 16 frames, where a playback encode has one every few seconds), no audio track, and a separate portrait encode for phones. A normal web encode plays fine and scrubs badly. Keep a poster visible until a real frame has painted, and make it the film's first frame as the page renders it: re-shoot it after any change to the look, and force the first seek even when the target equals `currentTime`, or a poster cut from another frame stays up at the start. iOS leaves a muted video blank until it has played once, so prime each scrubbed film at `loadedmetadata`: call `play()`, call `pause()` once the play promise resolves (a pause in the same task aborts the play before a frame decodes), and retry on the first touch end or click (a touch `pointerdown` does not count as a user activation). Measure before choosing the spacing. On one page the desktop copy of a scrubbed film (1080p) went from a keyframe every 8 frames to one every 16: the median and the per-notch seek figures held (16 ms at the median), the file was 17.5 percent smaller, and the worst small forward step grew from 66 to 100 ms, which the reviewer could not see. The phone copy stayed at one every 4, because it had not been timed on a real phone.
- **Ground or greet.** A pinned section's first content must be visible when the stage enters: either a ground that already carries the screen, or a first copy state that starts at full opacity. The hero headline is present at load.
- **Only the last section holds.** A middle section whose final copy state never fades can ride up through the next section and under fixed chrome. Close every state except the page's last.
- **Clip paths follow the box, not the ink.** Wiping tight display type (line-height under 1) shears ascenders and descenders. Wipe a padded wrapper instead.
- **One writer per transform.** Magnet, parallax, and entrance rises all write `transform`. Give each its own element.
- **Moving planes transition opacity only.** A `transition: transform` on a parallaxed plane makes it lag the thumb on phones.
- **Pan rails crop copy.** Card headings are read half-visible at the edges; keep them to one or two short words.
- **Depth layers run on film time.** Particles, snow, dust, or bubbles laid over a scrubbed film take their motion from the film's playhead, not from raw scroll. They then hold still where the film holds a frame, and keep the film's pace where the scroll-to-time map speeds up or slows down. Driven by raw scroll, they stream past a frozen frame and show where the map stalls.
- **One progress model drives every readout.** Map the film, any drawn line, readouts, counts, and step labels through one table of event landmarks: mapped straight to progress, one film showed its main event 66 seconds early by the page's own clock. Round a readout by the same rule as the state boundaries, count an item only once it is fully drawn and uncovered, and let the copy state a figure only once the element on screen shows it.
- **A marker that lives in the film gets its own start, read off the film.** When labels or plates appear as something in a scrubbed film reaches them (water leaving a spot, a light arriving), give each one the frame at which its own spot changes, for each layout, since a wide crop and an upright crop show different spots. An even step fails because filmed motion is not even: on one tide film the water took 9 to 13 frames from one spot to the next through the middle of a slip and 3 to 5 at its far end, so with an even step the last plate stood up about ten frames after its spot had dried. Read the frames by eye over a coarse grid (a 10 percent grid laid on screenshots at set frames); an automatic detector was too noisy on water to use. Keep the positions in the film's own coordinates, so a scaled copy for phones cannot move them.
- **Scrim the words, not the frame.** Text over a film or scene that changes brightness gets a soft scrim anchored to its own words: a blurred plate on the text block, or a `::before` inside the element that carries the text's slide, so the scrim moves with it. A gradient keyed to fixed fractions of the screen misses the words once their position depends on the window's height (a phone date line over one read 1.93 to 3.05:1), and a text-shadow glow alone lifted contrast about 1.3 times and never reached 4.5:1.
- **Thin the drawing under the words, in the canvas.** The plate above stays for films and photographs. When the page itself draws a 2D canvas behind the text, do the scrim's work in the drawing: measure the text boxes and take the drawing under them down to about a quarter with a few soft `destination-out` passes, then paint the ground behind everything with `destination-over`. A CSS scrim over the same canvas showed as a dark blotch at the scene's bright hour and still let lines through at its weak edge, where one label read 2.3:1.
- **A bright band in a drawn ground starts below the words.** Place a horizon glow or any lighter band from the measured lower edge of the lowest text box, not at a fixed fraction of the stage. In a short window a list wrapped onto a dusk band placed by fraction, and its last entry read 3.63:1.
- **One instrument per reading.** Two gauges on a page never show the same unit with different values. If a section needs an instrument of its own, give it its own quantity (the room in degrees while the page-long gauge reads pressure), so the page-long reading keeps its payoff at the end.
- **Check where each move ends against what the next one does.** When a new move reaches a pose that a later move was written to reach, the later move has nothing left to do. A travel added before a closing push turned one object to the push's end pose, so the push, described as turning a little as it goes, did not turn at all on windows wide enough for the travel. Re-read each move against where the one before it leaves the object, and say in the spec which move does the turning.
- **An object walks in and out at full strength.** An entry or an exit drawn as a long fade leaves a see-through double of the object on screen: on one page a walking shoe faded in and out over whole strides, and at 1024 x 768 a ghost was on screen for about half of the section's scroll. Put the spots before the first stop and after the last far enough past the window's edges that the object is out of sight, move it to and from them at full opacity, and keep any fade short and at the edge. Probe every 0.005 of the section and count the samples whose set opacity is neither 0 nor 1: the old build had 55 to 105 at three window sizes, and the fix had none at twelve.
- **Place moving parts in the free space you measured.** Offsets set as shares of a box, or tracks at fixed fractions of a canvas, hold at the size they were tuned at and meet the words at others. Pieces spread by shares of a box that grew with the window's height crossed a paragraph at 1440 x 1300. Two walking tracks at fixed fractions of their canvas put the upper shoe under a heading on short laptop windows, where the page's rule against crossing words held that shoe back, so one shoe walked alone for 48 to 66 percent of the walk at 1280 x 650, 1280 x 720, 1366 x 657, 1366 x 768, 1440 x 760 and 1536 x 730, while 1024 x 768, 1440 x 900, 1600 x 900, 1920 x 1080 and 2560 x 1080 showed none of it. Take each lane from the measured ink around it (from the heading's ink bottom to the top of the figures below, say), clamp every piece inside it, and shrink the pieces when the lane cannot hold them.
- **Give a travelling object a lane.** An object that travels down the page past text needs a column that the layout keeps empty in every section it passes, so it never has to be steered round the words. One page kept a 220px middle column clear through three sections, and a sweep of the travel found text behind the object only where the design put it there.
- **One object travels by moving the copies already in the page.** To carry an instrument from the first screen up into the header and down into the closing band, transform the hero's copy toward the header's from the scroll position alone, and cross-fade the two over the last stretch of the flight (one page used the last seventh). Lift its stacking order only once it is clear of what it passed under, and test that against the visible part of that element. One flight tested a card's whole box, which reached up under the header, so on upright windows from 1024px wide the instrument slid under the header for a few frames before it rose over it, with up to 41px hidden.
- **The last resting screen clears the header.** When something in the close finishes only at the foot of the page, the page's last screen is the one the visitor rests on, and on laptop heights its heading can sit under a fixed header. One close's heading top sat at 3, -12 and -29px at 1440 x 900, 1366 x 768 and 1024 x 768, its first line under the header; another's was 30px under the bar at 1100 x 640 and 20px under at 1280 x 650. Read the page at its maximum scroll at laptop heights, and end the scrub when the close reaches the header, or shrink the close on short windows.

## Layered heroes

For a premium marketing hero, depth is planned before assets are made:

1. Name the planes back to front: background, focal subject, foreground, optional atmosphere.
2. Build real compositing assets: a clean background plate with the subject removed, genuine alpha cutouts (check the alpha channel, not a fake checkerboard), and shared contact points that stay anchored.
3. Move planes at visibly different but restrained rates. Pause offscreen work. Load the full layer set before swapping out the poster.
4. Art-direct mobile separately: crop, subject position, type order, and travel all change. Type may sit above the subject on a phone even if it passes behind it on desktop.
5. Reduced motion keeps a complete static composition with depth and no extra pinned scroll.

Type that the object crosses still has to read. When a headline's first word is set giant behind the object and dropped from the visible heading, the object can cover the one word the sentence needs: at 1440 x 900 one blade left an S, half an H and EN of a seven-letter verb, and the first screen no longer said what was sold. Put the giant word in front of the object with only the letter its edge runs through masked, or keep the word in the heading. Mask the letters and give their box the shadow, because a filter runs before a mask on the same element, and a masked letter otherwise leaves its neighbours' shadows cut off square. A round object cannot rest across a long headline either: behind one gauge a 61-character headline lost whole words, and a tuck of a few pixels read as a mistake. That page shortened the headline to five words, kept the gauge clear of every line at rest (18px or more, measured), and let it cross the type in motion.

Respect explicit static or simpler direction. Working surfaces such as dashboards do not need an invented hero.

## The signature move

Each scroll page earns one bespoke interaction that belongs to this product alone: a line drawing that builds from scroll progress, a running ledger of real figures that totals by the close, one control that regrades the entire page. A tuned parameter on an existing device is not a signature move. Describe it to someone who has seen your other work; if they cannot tell it apart, it is not one.

A signature that answers the visitor (a score, a slider, a question) keeps its answer on screen with the control that drives it, at every width. On a phone, measure how much of the feedback element is visible while the question and its button are in view. Anything under 100% is a defect: the visitor acts and sees nothing happen. One build measured 28% on a phone, and it stayed a defect until a phone layout brought it to 100%.

A pointer-driven signature also works under a finger and from the keyboard. Set `touch-action: pan-y` so a sideways drag paints while a vertical one scrolls (`none` only where the effect owns the screen), let a focusable slider drive the same effect, and word the hint for the input in use (drag on touch, move the pointer with a mouse), hiding only a hover-only hint under `(hover: none)`.

Before building, compare the plan against your earlier builds on six axes: grammar, nav, hero device, section sequence, close, signature move. Differ on at least four. If the plan fails, change the plan.

## Native scroll-driven CSS

When one scroll-linked pattern is enough, prefer CSS scroll timelines over a library.

- One scroll-linked pattern per page, on structure only: a pinned chapter, one display element, a progress hairline, a sticky diptych. Prose never moves under the reader.
- Base CSS is the finished layout. The choreography lives inside `@supports (animation-timeline: view())` and `@media (prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference) and (min-width: 40rem)`, so unsupported browsers, small screens, and reduced motion get the complete page.
- Declare `animation-timeline` and `animation-range` after the `animation` shorthand, which resets them.
- Use `linear` timing for scrubbed progress; easing detaches the page from the thumb.
- Elements inside a stuck pane cannot drive their own `view()` timeline. Put a named `view-timeline` on the tall track and slice it with `animation-range`.
- The only fallback is reveal-once with IntersectionObserver, hidden only under a `.js` class. Never a scroll-listener polyfill.

```css
.chapter { min-height: 280vh; }
.chapter__pin { position: sticky; top: var(--space-xl); }
@supports (animation-timeline: view()) {
  @media (prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference) and (min-width: 40rem) {
    .chapter { view-timeline: --chapter block; }
    .chapter li { animation: step-in linear both; animation-timeline: --chapter; }
    .chapter li:nth-child(1) { animation-range: contain 5% contain 30%; }
    .chapter li:nth-child(2) { animation-range: contain 30% contain 55%; }
    .chapter li:nth-child(3) { animation-range: contain 55% contain 80%; }
  }
}
@keyframes step-in { from { opacity: 0.2; transform: translateY(8px); } to { opacity: 1; transform: none; } }
```

Reach for GSAP (`skills/gsap-scroll-choreography`) when several media, copy, and scene states must be coordinated on one timeline.

## Pinned stages, hand-overs, and reveals

Rules from review rounds on pages that pin a live stage and swap a still for a live model.

- **Reparenting can replay an entrance.** Removing a node and inserting it somewhere else restarts its CSS animations; only `moveBefore()` keeps their state. Before a script moves an animated hero into a pinned wrapper, remove the entrance class, and make sure the element's resting style is its final state.
- **Hand over from the still in the still's own pose.** Render the still from the same code and camera as the live model. Fade the live canvas in over the still and remove the still only once it is covered. Give the model no turn-in of its own on take-over, and hold pointer input until the still has gone. Then measure it: compare the frame before take-over with the frame after, with the pointer still, and treat a visible jump as a defect. The same goes for a fixed layer that stands in for a still picture. Swap only once every file the layer draws has decoded, and keep the still on any failure: with its pieces added on load, a 404 on either piece left a broken-image frame where one page's only object should be. Place the layer on the still's own rectangle in the same task that shows it and swap with no fade: a fade-in that began after the still had painted made the object show, vanish and come back on every wide load, gone for half a second on a slow line.
- **A sticky stage is a stacking context.** Everything inside it stacks as one layer, so a fixed layer outside paints over all of it or under all of it. A page-wide canvas fixed at the root drew a cord over the paper panel inside a sticky stage that used to hide it, and on 360 x 640 and 320 x 568 phones the cord lay across the hero's main button at load. To set one fixed object between two layers of a held section, make each layer its own sticky grid item in one cell, with stacking orders below and above the object; a sticky wrapper round both cannot do it. Where the canvas has to stay above, clip it out of the panel's box while the panel shows, and shut the clip at once when the panel comes back: a clip that slid shut over 0.3s left the cord on the returning button for about a quarter of a second.
- **Controls in a pinned stage stay reachable on short screens.** A pinned stage with a minimum height taller than the screen, as on a phone held sideways, can clip its own controls, and focus then lands on something the visitor cannot see. Keep the pin to the screen and cut its copy to the heading and controls, move the rest outside the pin, or drop the pin at that size. A pinned box with `overflow: hidden` passes only when its `scrollHeight` equals its `clientHeight`; one panel's last row sat below an 800px screen with nothing able to bring it into view.
- **A held stage clips its overflow.** Keyboard focus scrolls a box with `overflow: hidden` to bring the focused element into view, and the box stays scrolled. Tabbing between the links of a sideways row moved one stage 893, 1785 and 2678px, and after focus left it stayed 447px off, every item shifted and the lit one cut at the edge, until a reload. Use `overflow: clip`, which focus cannot scroll, and on `focusin` scroll the page to the focused item's own stop, since with the clip alone the focused link stayed off screen. Tab through every new control and read the stage's `scrollLeft`.
- **The peak pins on the screens people use.** A stage that pins only when its content fits one screen can lose its pin on most phones (390 x 664 with the browser bars showing, 375 x 667, 360 x 640, any phone held sideways) and on 1366 x 768 laptops, so size its type and controls in `svh` until it pins down to about 360 x 640 and 1280 x 600. On short and turned phones, drop secondary panels and rows before cards cover the subject: opaque cards hid 85 to 95% of one 3D model at 375 x 667. Design the unpinned version as a composition of its own, since a cover film stretched behind a long list crops into blur.
- **Measure a pinned stage with its hold off.** Once a stage holds, its box is the screen, so a test for "fits one screen" always passes and the pin never lets go after a resize or rotation. Remove the hold, read the height, and restore the hold in the same task, with `overflow-anchor: none` on the section so the swap cannot move the scroll, and measure again on every resize.
- **Pinned-layout rules stay in the pinned layout.** Scope every sticky offset, stage height, and absolute placement to the live mode and the widths it serves (a selector such as `html.live` that only that mode carries), because `.js` stays on the root under reduced motion and specificity outranks media order: leaked rules left about 4,900px of empty scroll on one phone and clipped a finished stage to 12px. Reset `top: auto` wherever a sticky or absolute box goes relative, and never give a sticky layer a negative bottom margin, which lets it run a full screen into the next section. After any change to a sticky rule, scroll through the pinned span and look: moving `position: sticky` onto a shared class let a later rule of equal weight unpin one stage and leave four screens of empty ground.
- **Keep what a run shows apart from what the visitor picked.** A scroll run that steps through colours or options changes its own words and highlight only. The order summary, the form, and aria-checked stay on the visitor's pick. Test at the scroll position where the run's last screen and the order button are both in view.
- **Keep the place through reloads and geometry changes.** When a script builds the page's height after load, the browser's own restore can land short. If you measure that, set `history.scrollRestoration` to manual. The page is then responsible for reload and Back alike: save the position on leaving, restore it after the build, and build at once when there is a place to restore. Turn scroll anchoring off (`overflow-anchor: none`) where a script adds a pin's height, or the browser adds it a second time (one reload landed five screens further down); hold a pinned stage's progress whenever its length changes, so a rotation keeps the frame; scroll a hash target into place again once the pin is built; and refresh a scroll library after the browser restores the position (`ScrollTrigger.refresh()`). On a touch device, rebuild a pin's length only when the width changes or the height moves by more than about 120px, since a phone fires resize as its toolbar hides; re-measuring placements takes no threshold (`layout.md`). A pinned section drawn from a tween also draws its progress when the scroll library refreshes (an `onRefresh` that renders the trigger's progress): without it, a fresh load on a link further down left one roast at its start, its clock at 0:00 and its curve undrawn, when the visitor scrolled back up. Or take held sections' heights from CSS alone (a class the head sets, plus a media query), so links, hash loads and reloads need no correction: one page built that way came back on the same frame after reloads at four depths.
- **Smooth scrolling for chosen navigation only.** With CSS smooth scrolling on for the whole page, a Tab that moves focus far down the page animates there, and the focused field can be off screen while it travels. Scroll smoothly from a link's own click, leave focus scrolling immediate, and skip the animation under reduced motion. On a page with a measured pin, smooth root scrolling also breaks the pin, because a scroll library that re-measures by scrolling lands its measurements mid-scroll: after a reload or a resize, one stage pinned over the hero or left three empty screens. A page that keeps CSS smooth scrolling on the root also changes a reload: with only sticky content on screen, Chrome restored one held stage's position by a smooth glide from the top of the page. On such a page, switch the root's smooth scrolling on with a class about 300ms after load, and test a reload at a step by reading the position at once (that page then showed its step 150ms after the reload).
- **A reveal fails open.** Hide reveal targets only after the observer has reported once, and run the reveal from a small script of its own. If the observer never calls back, or a later script throws, everything stays visible. A target that takes focus shows at once.
- **Reduced motion is a finished, still page.** Under reduced motion and with scripting off, unpin the sequence and show its end state: every step listed, films on their first frame and never fetched for scrubbing, eased values at their targets, runways down to one screen, and no instruction or control that would do nothing there ("Scroll to run", an animate toggle). The override undoes every property the motion used to hide (opacity, visibility, clipping, stacking). Add `autoplay` from script once motion is allowed, since the attribute plays with scripting off and ignores reduced motion.
- **Text can move in on wide screens while the stage stays.** On a full-bleed film or canvas, elements placed against the picture (a rail, callouts) belong to the picture's edge. If the text column moves toward the centre on wide screens, give it a gutter token of its own, because a shared one can carry an overlay across the thing it annotates. Check each overlay against the picture at every width.
- **The stage marks itself.** Set the class that turns on a stage's pinned layout from the stage's own script, after it has its canvas and context, never from a script in the head. A head class stays on when the stage script fails: on one phone layout that showed only the current entry, a failed script then showed none. Marked by its own script, a stage that fails stays the plain section. Keep the page's height off that class, though. Rules that change how tall the page is hang on a class the head script sets, because Chrome can restore a reload's scroll before a later script adds its class: one stage grew from 2,003 to 3,600px when its late class landed, scroll anchoring followed the content below it down by 1,597px, and a reload in the last 44 percent of the stage reopened on the next section. That has a cost. Both pages that put their held sections on a head class put the whole held layout there with the height, so a stage whose script fails keeps its pinned height and its held layout with nothing moving in it, and no longer falls back to the plain section. On one of them, in the nearest state measured (its script running, its pictures never loaded), the names sat at their CSS size and each cut-out was parked over the middle of its name.
- **Steps keep their stretch of scroll in every mode.** The spacing between steps beside a sticky panel is layout, not motion, and so is the panel when CSS `position: sticky` alone holds it. Keep both with motion reduced and with scripting off. The rules above that unpin a sequence and scope sticky offsets to the live mode are for a scrubbed pin and its runway, and a panel held by CSS alone has neither. Packed, five steps sat on one screen, and the panel had nothing to hold beside.
- **Hold the first step.** Place the line that lights a step so the second step reaches it most of a step's scroll after the pin starts (a third of the way down the window did it). With the line at the middle, the first step's state flashed past within 24px of the pin.
- **Words over a held stage hold with it.** A list that scrolls over a pinned room lets each lit step's words rise under the header before the next step lights: one room showed no step words at all for 36 percent of its pinned scroll at 1440 x 900, 32 percent at 1100 x 640 and 38 percent at 1920 x 1080. Put each step's words in a sticky holder with no height of its own, the words in an inner box, so they hold on one line from the moment the step reaches it until the step's end does, and light the step from the holder's own computed `top`. At the change, take the old words out before the new ones come in, or cut at once: two holders on one line, cross-fading over 0.3s, drew both steps' words over each other at every step change. The last step's words leave with the stage.
- **Walk a stage on its way in and on its way out.** Its edges need the same walk as its hold, on a phone held sideways too. Held sideways, one stage's captions printed across its glowing lamps; on the way out it scrolled up under a see-through nav, and its heading printed over the nav's links. Where a stage follows a film, feather its top edge into the film, or the flat edge cuts across the film's last picture (one page used a gradient 20 to 30vh tall). A feather that rises ahead of the stage eats into the hold before it, so count it in the hold: a 30vh feather left a 150vh hold exactly at its minimum, and 20vh gave it 160vh.
- **Dim a waiting step by colour.** Entries waiting at 30 percent opacity read 1.8:1 over a drawn sky. Give each state its own colour that clears 4.5:1 on the brightest ground it can meet, and measure every state on the composited page.
- **A line the scroll draws needs no pin.** Tie its dash offset to the drawing's own travel up the window. Tied to its section's, a chart that sat 400px down the section had 30 to 42 percent of its line drawn before its top showed on desktop screens, so the rise the chart was there to show drew unseen. Start when the corner where the line begins is on screen (in ScrollTrigger terms `start: "90% bottom"` on the chart, which is the point 90 percent down the chart meeting the window's bottom; that chart's line began just above that point, so set the percentage to where your own line starts) and end as the chart's middle reaches the window's, so every stroke lands in view. A visitor who stops with the section at rest may then see the line part drawn (60 to 78 percent on desktop screens, and whole a tenth of a screen later); let a dot and a figure ride the line's end while it is, so it reads as in progress. Ending at the rest position instead would leave about 170 to 230px of scroll for the whole drawing in a 1440px window, which would read as a jump. Keep the chart shorter than the window, with a cap on a phone held sideways, or its first stroke and its last cannot both be watched. With motion reduced or scripting off the line is whole and the dot is absent.

## Verify by scrolling

A scroll page has no single state. Check it mechanically and by eye:

- Sample several positions inside each section, not uniformly down the document, so findings do not move when an unrelated section changes height. For a held section, walk its whole pinned scroll in small steps (16px) at many window sizes and count the positions with a whole lit block on screen, then prove the walk can fail by breaking the hold on purpose: with its holders unstuck, one stage's count fell from 100 to 27.6 percent at 1440 x 900.
- Look for dead scroll (nothing changes across consecutive samples), frozen clips (a stage moves but its playhead does not), copy states that never reach full opacity, and contrast measured against the brightest frame under each line. Count the film frames between presented frames with `requestVideoFrameCallback`: an eased playhead moves one or two a step on a wheel notch, where writing the target directly cut nine.
- Measure contrast on the composited page with the text hidden by its colour (not its container, or the probe reads the film instead of the text's plate): sample a thin ring around the glyphs of every line not under a fixed header, and grade light text against the brightest 5% of that ring and dark text against the darkest 2%. Sample several moments of each loop, a live scene at fixed phases of its clock, and text while it slides in or out; one sample caught a loop at its darkest and missed labels under 3:1. Repeat in each theme, under reduced motion, and in short laptop windows (1366 x 650, 1280 x 650, 1536 x 730).
- Serve over http and use a browser with the codecs the media needs; `file://` and codec-less Chromium silently fall back to posters and prove nothing.
- Review a contact sheet of the samples at desktop, phone, and reduced motion.
- Run a cold feel check: scroll once at reading pace, write one word per section, then compare to the planned curve. Where they disagree, the page is wrong.
- Say what was not covered. Headless Chrome does not reproduce a real phone's decoder, autoplay policy, Low Power Mode, or touch scrolling.

Resolve and Vision (`resolve.md`, `vision.md`) still own the mechanical floor and the rendered review.
